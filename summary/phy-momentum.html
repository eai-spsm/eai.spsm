Momentum & Impulse

Physics for Engineering · M.4 · Chapter 7 / 7

Momentum as mass times velocity, impulse as the change in momentum, conservation of momentum, and what separates elastic from inelastic collisions.

On this page

  2. Impulse
  3. Conservation of momentum
  4. Types of collisions
  5. Worked examples

Linear momentum

Momentum is mass times velocity: p = mv. It is a vector, pointing the way the velocity points. The unit is kg·m/s, which equals N·s. Heavier or faster objects carry more momentum, and mass and speed count equally.

ObjectMassSpeedMomentum
Loaded truck5000 kg20 m/s100 000 kg·m/s
Tennis ball0.06 kg60 m/s3.6 kg·m/s
Bullet0.01 kg400 m/s4 kg·m/s

Newton's second law is really ΣF = Δp/Δt: net force is the rate of change of momentum. This is more general than F = ma because it still works when mass changes, as for a rocket burning fuel. F = ma is the special case of constant mass.

Impulse

Impulse is force times the time it acts: J = FΔt. The impulse–momentum theorem says it equals the change in momentum: J = Δp = m(v − u). A big force for a short time and a small force for a long time can give the same change in momentum.

ConceptFormulaUnit
ImpulseJ = FΔtN·s = kg·m/s
Impulse–momentum theoremJ = Δp = mv − mu—
Average forceF̄ = Δp/ΔtN

Why it matters

Airbags stretch the stopping time, cutting the peak force for the same Δp. A cricket follow-through lengthens contact with the ball, adding impulse and speed. Catching a ball with hands pulled back lengthens Δt and softens the blow. Double Δt in Fig. 1 and the average force halves.

Conservation of momentum

In an isolated system (no net external force) total momentum is conserved: momentum before a collision equals momentum after.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂u = before, v = after, signs show direction

Where it comes from

Newton's third law gives F₁₂ = −F₂₁ for the same time Δt, so m₁Δv₁ = −m₂Δv₂, i.e. m₁(v₁ − u₁) = −m₂(v₂ − u₂), which rearranges to the equation above.

ScenarioEquation
General collisionm₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
Object 2 at rest (u₂ = 0)m₁u₁ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
Perfectly inelastic (stick)m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v
Explosion (starts at rest)0 = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Types of collisions

Momentum is conserved in every isolated collision. Kinetic energy may or may not be. That is what sorts collisions into types.

v₁ = (m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) × u₁    v₂ = 2m₁/(m₁ + m₂) × u₁
MassesResult
m₁ = m₂v₁ = 0, v₂ = u₁: full transfer of momentum and KE
m₁ ≫ m₂v₁ ≈ u₁, v₂ ≈ 2u₁: the heavy one barely slows, the light one flies off at double speed

Worked examples

Gun recoil (an explosion)

A 0.5 kg gun fires a 0.01 kg bullet at 300 m/s. Starting at rest, total momentum is 0, so 0 = 0.01 × 300 + 0.5 × vgun, giving vgun = −3/0.5 = −6 m/s: the gun recoils at 6 m/s opposite the bullet.

Kinetic energy lost when objects stick

m₁ = 2 kg at 5 m/s hits m₂ = 3 kg at rest. KE before = ½(2)(25) = 25 J. vafter = (2 × 5)/(2 + 3) = 2 m/s. KE after = ½(5)(4) = 10 J. KE lost = 15 J, turned into heat, sound and deformation.

def collision(m1, u1, m2, u2=0, elastic=True):
    if elastic:
        v1 = ((m1-m2)*u1 + 2*m2*u2) / (m1+m2)
        v2 = ((m2-m1)*u2 + 2*m1*u1) / (m1+m2)
    else:
        v1 = v2 = (m1*u1 + m2*u2) / (m1+m2)
    return v1, v2
print(collision(6, 4, 2))                 # (2.0, 6.0)
print(collision(4, 3, 2, elastic=False))  # (2.0, 2.0)
