Inclined Plane

Physics for Engineering · M.4 · Chapter 3 / 7

Splitting gravity into a component along the slope and a component into it, which unlocks friction, acceleration and the angle at which a block starts to slide.

  2. Normal force
  3. Friction on an incline

Force components on an incline

A block on a slope at angle θ has weight mg pointing straight down. Working in global horizontal and vertical directions is awkward, so resolve mg along the slope's own two axes:

Parallel to the slope: mg sin θacts down the slope. It is the force trying to make the block slide, and grows as the slope gets steeper.
Perpendicular to the slope: mg cos θpresses the block into the surface and is balanced by the normal force N when there is no acceleration across the slope.

As θ goes from 0° to 90°, mg sin θ rises from 0 to mg and mg cos θ falls from mg to 0. On a flat surface (θ = 0°) nothing drives a slide. On a vertical wall (θ = 90°) the whole weight drives it and there is no normal force at all. Drag the angle in Fig. 1 and watch the dashed grey components change.

Normal force

N is always perpendicular to the surface. A frequent error is to say N = mg always. That is only true on a flat horizontal surface; on a slope N balances the component pressing into it.

SurfaceNormal forceCondition
Flat horizontalN = mgfull weight presses down
Incline at θN = mg cos θno acceleration across the slope
Incline, extra push-down FN = mg cos θ + F sin θextra load into the surface
Incline, extra pull-up FN = mg cos θ − F sin θlift reduces contact force

If N = 0 the block has lifted off the surface.

Friction on an incline

Friction acts along the surface and opposes motion or the tendency to slide. Since N is mg cos θ, friction is also smaller than on flat ground: f = μN = μmg cos θ.

TypeConditionFormulaNote
Static fsblock not movingfs ≤ μsNadjusts up to a maximum μsN
Kinetic fkblock slidingfk = μkNconstant once sliding; μk < μs

Critical angle. The block is just about to slide when the driving component equals maximum static friction: mg sin θc = μs mg cos θc, so tan θc = μs and θc = arctan μs. Tilting a surface until an object slips is a simple way to measure μs.

In the diagram the panel shows the critical angle for the chosen μ. Move θ above it and the state flips from "stays put" to "sliding".

Acceleration down the slope

Take down the slope as positive. Net force = driving component − friction: Fnet = mg sin θ − μk mg cos θ. Divide by m:

a = g ( sin θ − μk cos θ )

If μ cos θ ≥ sin θ the formula would go negative: friction is strong enough to prevent sliding, so the block stays at rest under static friction and a = 0.

CaseAcceleration
No friction (μ = 0)a = g sin θ
Sliding down with frictiona = g(sin θ − μk cos θ)
Pushed up the slope, then slowinga = g(sin θ + μk cos θ) directed down the slope: gravity and friction both act downhill

Worked example

A 10 kg block on a 30° slope with μk = 0.25 slides freely from rest, starting 5 m up the slope. Find the acceleration and the time to reach the bottom.

StepWorking
1 Normal forceN = mg cos 30° = 10 × 10 × (√3/2) ≈ 86.6 N
2 Kinetic frictionfk = 0.25 × 86.6 ≈ 21.7 N
3 Net force along slopemg sin 30° − fk = 50 − 21.7 = 28.3 N
4 Accelerationa = 28.3/10 = 2.83 m/s²
5 Time to the bottoms = ½at²: 5 = ½(2.83)t² → t² ≈ 3.53 → t ≈ 1.88 s

Set θ = 30, m = 10, μ = 0.25 in Fig. 1: the panel shows N = 86.6 N, friction 21.65 N and a = 2.83 m/s², matching the steps.

import math
def incline(m, theta_deg, mu_k=0, g=10):
    th = math.radians(theta_deg)
    N = m*g*math.cos(th)
    f = mu_k*N
    a = g*(math.sin(th) - mu_k*math.cos(th))
    return N, f, a
print(incline(10, 30, 0.25))   # (86.6, 21.65, 2.83)
