Newton's Laws & Free Body Diagrams

Physics for Engineering · M.4 · Chapter 2 / 7

The three laws that connect force to motion, and the free body diagram: the drawing habit that makes every force problem solvable.

On this page
  1. First law: inertia
  2. Second law: F = ma
  3. Third law: action and reaction
  4. Free body diagrams
  5. Worked example: the Atwood machine

First law: inertia

Newton's first law. An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. When ΣF = 0 the object is in equilibrium.

ScenarioStateNet force
Book on a tableat restzero: weight balanced by normal force
Car at constant speed on a straight roadmoving, not acceleratingzero: engine force equals resistance
Hockey puck on frictionless iceconstant velocityzero

It is not natural for things to stop. The natural state of any object is constant velocity, and zero is just one such velocity. Friction is the everyday force that brings things to rest and hides this law.

Second law: F = ma

ΣF = m anet force (N = kg·m/s²) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m/s²)

The acceleration points the same way as the net force. Double the net force and the acceleration doubles; double the mass and it halves. The resistance of a mass to changes in its motion is inertia. Try it in Fig. 1: push with F, add mass, then add friction.

Notice the two stages. While F is smaller than the maximum friction μN, static friction exactly matches F and the block does not move. Once F exceeds μN the block accelerates with a = (F − μN)/m.

Third law: action and reaction

Newton's third law. If object A pushes on B with force F, then B pushes on A with an equal and opposite force: FA→B = −FB→A. The two forces act on different objects.

ActionReaction
Earth pulls you down (gravity)you pull the Earth up, equally
You push a wall to the rightthe wall pushes you to the left
A rocket expels gas downwardthe gas pushes the rocket up (thrust)
Your foot pushes the ground backthe ground pushes your foot forward (walking)
Common misconceptionAction and reaction do not cancel, because they act on different objects. For ΣF = 0 on one object you need forces on that same object to cancel, and an action–reaction pair never does that.

Free body diagrams

A free body diagram (FBD) isolates one object and shows every force on it as an arrow starting at its centre. It makes hidden forces visible and prevents sign errors; do it before any equation.

StepAction
1 Isolatedraw the object alone, as a box or a dot
2 Identifylist the forces on it: weight (down), normal (perpendicular to the surface), tension (along the rope), friction (opposing motion), applied force, air resistance
3 Drawone arrow per force, length proportional to size
4 Labelsymbol and value beside each arrow
5 Sumapply ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may separately
ForceDirectionFormula
Weight Walways downW = mg
Normal Nperpendicular to the surfaceN = mg on level ground; N = mg cos θ on an incline
Tension Talong the string, toward the anchorgiven, or from ΣF
Static friction fsopposes the tendency to slidefs ≤ μsN
Kinetic friction fkopposes the motionfk = μkN

Worked example: the Atwood machine

Two masses hang over a light, frictionless pulley on a light, inextensible string, so both share the same acceleration size and the same tension T. Take m₁ = 3 kg, m₂ = 5 kg, g = 10 m/s².

ObjectFBD equationResult
m₂ = 5 kg (heavier, falls)m₂g − T = m₂a → 50 − T = 5aequation ①
m₁ = 3 kg (lighter, rises)T − m₁g = m₁a → T − 30 = 3aequation ②
① + ②20 = 8aa = 2.5 m/s²
TensionT = 30 + 3(2.5)T = 37.5 N

General results: a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂) and T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁ + m₂). Equal masses give a = 0, and a huge m₂ gives a → g (free fall).

def atwood(m1, m2, g=10):
    a = (m2 - m1) * g / (m1 + m2)
    T = m1 * (g + a)
    return a, T
print(atwood(3, 5))   # (2.5, 37.5)
